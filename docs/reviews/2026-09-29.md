The landing ran as `landq.sh` over a tier-ordered queue in batches of up to five stacked PRs, restarted by
hand after each stop. What the merge-commit queue and the batches taught, beyond pass 3:

- **Batching did not buy wall clock, and the median per PR is mostly queue position.** The queue builds
  one group per PR and runs them one at a time at 15 to 25 minutes each, so in a batch of five the first
  PR waits for its head checks and one group, the fifth for its head checks and five groups: 48, 65, 82,
  99, 116 minutes in the first tier-1 batch, a 17-minute step per position. The median PR is the third
  of five. Throughput is the number to compare, and it went the wrong way: 39 minutes per PR of wall
  clock against pass 3's 27, because every stop rebases the whole batch and re-runs every head's checks
  (25 to 45 minutes under the 20-job cap), and the tier-3 batch stopped five times. Three changes would
  recover it: enqueue each PR as soon as its own head is green rather than after the whole batch's (#691
  sat green for 26 minutes waiting for #704); let the queue build multi-PR groups so one CI run covers
  several merges; and resolve same-spot add/add hunks in any file, which were six of the nine hand
  stops. Recorded on #713.

- **A batch stops at its first conflict and the rest wait.** Six of the eight hand-resolves were two
  fixes adding text at the same spot of one file (a declaration block, a paragraph, a table, the point
  where new functions go), which `keep_both.py` handles only in the three list files. Each cost the batch
  one stop and a restart; the work was two minutes, the wait for the head checks was the rest. A
  same-spot insertion is the common case for parallel fixers of one module, and a resolver that keeps
  both sides of a pure add/add hunk in any file would have left two hand-resolves out of eight.
- **Gate every hand-resolve push on the tree, not the resolver.** My first resolution of #704 looked for
  a bare `$$;` line, left the markers in and pushed that head; the module install check caught it two
  minutes later. The rule now: a hand-resolved head is pushed only after `grep` finds no marker and the
  touched module installs.
- **The queue drops an enqueue during the mergeability recompute.** Right after a merge, a stacked PR's
  `mergeStateStatus` reads `UNKNOWN` for about a minute, and an enqueue in that window leaves no entry
  and no error. `land.sh` retried within 20 s and lost both; the retry should wait for the recompute.
- **A stuck entry looks like a slow one.** #699's second entry had all 29 checks green on its merge commit
  and sat `AWAITING_CHECKS` for 45 minutes; the queue's own timeout is 60. Dequeue and re-enqueue by
  hand is the only remedy, and the state to watch for is "every check green, no merge".
- **The storm again.** Twenty-four fixer heads pushed within an hour queued their PR runs behind the
  20-job cap and starved the record PR's merge-group summary jobs for 35 minutes. The queued (not
  running) PR-head runs of the tier-2 and tier-3 fix branches were cancelled twice (about 40 runs);
  every one of them was for a head the landing was about to rebase away. The `concurrency` group #644
  asks for is still the fix.

### Fixes are new surface: two interactions, both in code, and three instrument defects

- **#699 and #698 in the hypertable cutover's conservation check** (real code). C1 replaced the row count
  with a count plus a fingerprint of the rows; S5 (#654) had the same check refuse an untracked write the
  capture trigger missed, computed per relation so the MVCC horizon can be read. Merged by the
  coordinator: S5's per-relation scan keeps the count and the untracked-write refusal, the append-only
  path reads count and fingerprint in one scan, the tracking path reads the fingerprint in one more
  whole-table scan after the loop (the fingerprint must match the destination's through TimescaleDB's
  decompression), and the untracked-write refusal is ordered before the conservation comparison so a
  missed write is named as such. The `HT_CUTOVER_CONSERVATION` mutation constant now starts at the
  comparison. The timescale track passed on the merged tree before the push; a first attempt at the
  `mutate.py` conflict matched a marker-like substring inside a mutation's own description string, and
  was redone on whole-line markers.
- **#691 and #689 in the identity reseed** (real code, nine hunks). C2 reads the reseed's max and
  sequence position under the lock; S13 (#670) walks the sequence lattice with the sequence's options
  carried, and takes the minimum for a descending sequence. Delegated to #691's fixer with the merged
  design: the floors in the preflight, all three values refreshed under the lock, the lattice reseed
  from the refreshed values, and #689's two mutation patterns moved to the merged text. The gated suite
  passed on the result, all 185 mutations build, and #689's guards still discriminate. #701 and #703,
  which touch the same functions, were held back so they rebased onto #691 rather than the reverse.
- **`bench/throws_pinned.sh` under `pipefail`** (#714). The guard's control was asserted through
  `if echo "$out" | grep -q`; `grep -q` exits at its first match, the controls print before the sites,
  and once #688 took the site list past the pipe buffer (92 sites) `echo` died of EPIPE and the dead
  pipeline read as FAIL. Six guards shared the shape, four of them detecting FAILURE lines, where the
  race hides a failure instead. All six now read `grep -q PATTERN <<<"$out"`; the old shape fails
  deterministically with a 3 MB tail, the new one passes.
- **Test 160's clock** (#716). #697's test derived six uuidv7 ids from `date_trunc('minute', now())` in
  six separate statements; a run crossing a minute boundary between the first inserts and the one made
  during the regrain reorders two rows by id, and the two order-by-id assertions flip with every row
  present. It did so on #700's merge group on PostgreSQL 16 while 15, 17 and 18 passed the same tree
  seconds apart. The fixture now takes the instant once with `\gset`. Same lesson as pass 3's F2-07
  rebuild: a fixture's clock reads belong in one place.
- **A poll of `pg_stat_activity` that cannot observe change** (#717). `pg_stat_activity` is collected at
  its first read in a transaction and shown unchanged for the rest of it, the documented snapshot that only
  `pg_stat_clear_snapshot()` or transaction end discards. A plpgsql function is one transaction, so the
  `gone(pid)` poll in tests 116 and 166 read its first snapshot 600 times and returned true only when the
  terminated or disconnected backend had already left before that read; otherwise it spun for 30 s and
  failed its LIVENESS assertion. Three failures in one day, PostgreSQL 16 each time, read twice as load
  and retried. Confirmed on PG 15.19 with a dblink backend that stayed in the view after its disconnect
  until the snapshot was cleared; each turn now clears it first. The class is wider: every other plpgsql
  loop in `tests/` and `bench/` that polls the view has the same shape (tests 109, 157, 158, 162, 163 and
  the guards `maintain_lock.sh`, `regrain_outgoing_fk_lock.sh`, which is the #556 flake, and
  `retire_detach_lock.sh`); the ones keyed on `application_name` work because that column is fixed once
  the backend is in the snapshot, the ones keyed on `wait_event_type` or `state` pass only when the state
  was already there at the first read. Recorded on #713 for a follow-up that fixes them together with
  their discrimination proofs. It is CLAUDE.md's counters lesson from the other side: the counters flush
  at transaction end, and the activity view is read at transaction start.
